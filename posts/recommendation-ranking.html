From Model Scores to Recommendations: What I Learned About Ranking

A practical look at what happens between a recommendation model producing scores and a user actually seeing a ranked list.

Recommender Systems Ranking Evaluation ML Systems

While working with a neural news recommendation model, I initially thought the evaluation pipeline would be fairly straightforward: run the trained model, obtain scores for candidate news articles, sort them, and calculate a few metrics.

The interesting part turned out to be everything around the model. What exactly does a score represent? Which article is the ground-truth positive? How do we know that the prediction and label refer to the same candidate? And what does it actually mean when one model has a higher AUC but another has a better MRR?

A recommendation model produces scores, not recommendations

Suppose a user has a set of candidate news articles: 

News        Score

Article A   0.91
Article B   0.83
Article C   0.67
Article D   0.41

The model's output is not necessarily saying that Article A has a 91% probability of being clicked. Depending on the architecture and training objective, the number may simply be a relevance score.

What usually matters first is the ordering: 

A > B > C > D

In that sense, a recommendation model can be viewed as a ranking function: 

f(user, news) → score

The recommendation system then converts those scores into an ordered list.

Prediction and ground truth are different things

One distinction that became important to me was separating the model's prediction from the actual interaction recorded in the dataset. 

News        Score    Rank    Clicked

Article A   0.91       1        0
Article B   0.83       2        1
Article C   0.67       3        0
Article D   0.41       4        0

Here the model ranked Article A first, but the observed click was on Article B.

This is exactly where evaluation begins. The model provides the scores, while the dataset provides the labels. We need to align the two before calculating anything meaningful.

Candidate alignment matters more than it looks

Imagine that the model produces scores for: 

[N1, N2, N3, N4]

and the corresponding labels are: 

[0, 1, 0, 0]

The positive interaction belongs to N2.

If the labels accidentally become: 

[1, 0, 0, 0]

the evaluation pipeline now thinks N1 was the positive example. The model did not change. The metric did not change. Only the alignment between predictions and labels changed.

This made me pay much more attention to seemingly small details such as candidate ordering, dataset row identifiers, news IDs, and user context.

What does AUC actually tell us?

AUC is useful for measuring how well a model separates positive and negative candidates.

Conceptually, it asks:

If I randomly select one clicked article and one non-clicked article, how often does the model give the clicked article a higher score?

In simplified form: 

AUC = P(score_positive > score_negative)

This means AUC captures a different property from simply asking whether the first recommendation was correct.

MRR asks a different question

Mean Reciprocal Rank focuses on the position of the first relevant item. 

rank 1 → 1.00
rank 2 → 0.50
rank 3 → 0.33
rank 5 → 0.20

So MRR is particularly intuitive when we care about how quickly a recommendation system surfaces something relevant.

nDCG looks at the top of the whole ranking

nDCG takes the position of relevant items into account. An item near the top contributes more than the same item appearing much further down the list. 

DCG@k =
Σ (2^rel_i - 1) / log2(i + 1)

nDCG@k =
DCG@k / IDCG@k

This makes nDCG useful when we care about the quality of the complete top-k recommendation list rather than only the first relevant item.

Why I don't think one metric is enough

Imagine two models.

The first is very good at separating positive and negative candidates, but often puts the relevant article somewhere around rank 15. The second is slightly worse at overall separation but frequently places the relevant article in the top three.

Looking at only one metric can hide this difference.

AUC, MRR, and nDCG describe different properties of the ranking. Together they give a more useful picture of model behaviour.

Making prediction outputs easier to debug

One practical thing I found useful was keeping the prediction output explicit. 

dataset_row,user,news_id,score,rank,clicked
0,1,N5940,0.9131,1,0
0,1,N34130,0.8427,2,1
0,1,N15210,0.6312,3,0

A table like this connects several stages of the experiment: 

Dataset
   ↓
Candidate generation
   ↓
Model
   ↓
Scores
   ↓
Ranking
   ↓
Ground-truth alignment
   ↓
Metrics

It also makes debugging much easier. Instead of looking at an aggregate metric and wondering why it changed, I can inspect an individual recommendation list and ask what actually happened.

The small lesson I took away

Before working through this pipeline, I mostly thought about evaluation as the final step after training a model.

Now I think about it as part of the entire data flow.

A good recommendation experiment is not only about building a model that produces useful scores. It is also about maintaining the connection between the user, candidate item, prediction, ranking, and observed interaction all the way through evaluation.

In recommendation systems, the ranking is only part of the story. The other part is being able to explain exactly what was ranked, why it received that position, and how we know whether that ranking was useful.

This is one of those things that looks simple when written as “predict → rank → evaluate”, but becomes much more interesting once you start following the data through every step.
